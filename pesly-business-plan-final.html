1. The Problem: A Broken Trust Economy

Kenya's ride-hailing economy runs on an unspoken arrangement: an owner supplies the vehicle, a driver supplies the labour, and a fixed daily or weekly remittance travels between them. But the arrangement is built on numbers nobody can verify.

The owner sees what the platform reports — sometimes. The driver knows what they actually earned — and actually spent. Fuel prices move, commissions shift, cash trips vanish from records, payments reverse overnight. Between those two views sits a gap, and inside that gap lives the single largest source of conflict in the industry: the money argument.

It goes like this. The owner says: "You made more, remit more." The driver says: "You don't know my costs, my empty kilometres, my fuel, the three days this car sat in the garage." Both are telling the truth as they see it. Neither can prove anything. The argument repeats weekly, corrodes the relationship, and in the worst cases the driver quietly leaves with the owner's vehicle — or the owner quietly cuts the driver off from an income.

"Pesly does not create this conflict. The money gap creates it. Pesly only decides whether it is fought with evidence or with shouting."

The scale is real: Nairobi is one of the largest ride-hailing markets in Africa, with tens of thousands of vehicles operating under owner-driver arrangements. Every one of those pairings is a monthly money argument waiting for an honest referee.

2. The Solution: Evidence, Not Accusations

Pesly is an AI-first financial intelligence layer for vehicle-based work. Its core philosophy is a pipeline:

Collect evidence → Reconcile reality → Explain it → Create intelligence.

Unlike every bookkeeping app on the market, Pesly does not ask the driver to be its data-entry clerk. The AI is the primary data collector. It reads the same platform statements the owner could read, the same M-Pesa records the driver lives by, and it grades every figure by how it was obtained. When the driver does speak, they speak with evidence attached — and when they are wrongly accused, the record defends them.

Pesly is deliberately not a transport company, not a dispatch platform, and not a surveillance product. It is the trusted layer between two people who need to trust each other and currently cannot.

4. The Evidence Model

Every figure in Pesly carries two attributes: where it came from (log source) and how much it can be trusted (evidence status). No number appears in the app naked.

Log sources: gmail_sync · mpesa_sms · photo_ocr · manual · estimated

Evidence statuses: Verified · Supported · Reported · Estimated · Disputed · Unverified · Reconciled

A platform-statement figure is Verified. A driver's say-so is Reported. When the machine sources agree with each other, the figure becomes Reconciled — the only status both parties can fully rely on. When they disagree, the figure is flagged Disputed and enters the review queue with the raw evidence text attached (source_text), so any reviewer can see exactly what the claim is based on.

5. The Reconciliation Engine

The engine continuously cross-checks three independent views of the same money: what the platform reported, what the driver reported, and what M-Pesa actually moved. Mismatches automatically generate review alerts.

This is not a hypothetical feature. The engine is built and running in the current web app against seeded demo data, and it has already produced alerts of exactly this form:

"KSh 2,044 discrepancy on 2026-09-04 for KCA 234B: platform reported KSh 3,684, driver reported KSh 5,728. — Requires review."

Two design rules are absolute: the engine never accuses — it flags for human review, always in neutral language; and it works in both directions — it catches the forgotten remittance and the delayed platform payout with equal indifference. Most discrepancies turn out to be honest: a payment reversal, an unremitted cash trip, a late weekly statement. When the explanation is visible, an ugly weekly fight becomes a five-minute "oh, that's what happened."

6. The Agreement Engine

Most disputes are not about the numbers at all — they are about which numbers the deal applies to. Pesly encodes the owner-driver agreement itself:

TypeExample

Percentage30% of gross weekly fares
HybridKSh 1,500 base + 15% above KSh 6,000 gross

Every change creates a new versioned record — history is never overwritten. "We agreed 1,500" ends the day both sides can see Version 2, effective 1 September, signed by both, with the note: "Updated after fuel price increase."

7. The Trust Architecture

7.1 The central risk

The single greatest product risk is not technical — it is that drivers experience Pesly as the owner's surveillance app. If they do, they will refuse to connect their accounts, the data never arrives, and the product dies from the driver side. This plan treats that risk as the primary design constraint, not a footnote.

7.2 Consent-default-private

The driver, not the owner, controls the data boundary. On connect, the driver chooses — source by source — what flows to the owner (PrivacyGrant, an auditable consent trail aligned with the Kenya Data Protection Act 2019). The default share is only what the agreement needs: the remittance-relevant summary. Full trip detail, personal patterns, and spending outside the agreement stay the driver's unless they grant more. A driver who connects nothing shares nothing — which forces owners to sell Pesly to drivers as a benefit, not mandate it as a condition.

7.3 The owner sees the driver's costs too

Transparency is deliberately two-sided. In most Kenyan arrangements the owner already owns the platform account and sees the statements — what they never see is the driver's side: fuel, data, damages, idle days. Pesly shows both sides the same reconciled picture. Owners who discover costs they never accounted for renegotiate with facts instead of suspicion.

7.4 What the driver gets

Free, forever: their own complete financial record — true net earnings after every deduction, fuel costs, remittance history, dispute record, agreement versions. Plus one thing they cannot get anywhere else: a verified income certificate backed by machine evidence — accepted where a driver's word is not.

The driver pitch in one line: "The owner already sees what Bolt sends them. Pesly is the only place that shows your side — and proves it."

7.5 Who we accept losing

Honesty about who the product is not for: the driver quietly pocketing an extra KSh 1,500 a day will resist, and Pesly will lose them. The product is for the honest driver getting accused anyway — and there are far more of those. The onboarding rule follows from it: pilots run with owner-driver pairs who already have decent relationships, so the first experience of Pesly is a peace treaty, not a sting operation.

Why free core: the product's raw material is driver trust. Charging drivers to prove their own honesty would be self-defeating — the free tier is a customer-acquisition cost, not a missed revenue line. KSh 50 was evaluated and rejected as a Plus price: too low to signal value and marginal after AI processing costs. KSh 100 keeps the tier meaningful without gate-keeping dispute defence — which is the feature that wins drivers, and is therefore free.

Owner subscriptions remain the core revenue engine. The unit economics are attractive because AI does the collection labour: there is no bookkeeper on the payroll of either side.

13. Risks & Honest Answers

RiskHonest answer
Drivers reject it as surveillanceThe design constraint of the whole product (§7). Consent-default-private, driver-first value, pilot with trusting pairs. Some drivers will still refuse — we accept losing the drivers whose income depends on the gap.
Owners weaponize transparency in renegotiationThe data boundary limits what owners see without grants; costs flow to owners as visibly as earnings, rebalancing the information asymmetry rather than widening it.
Platforms change statement formats or APIsMulti-source design means no single point of failure; the evidence model degrades gracefully (a broken parser yields Unverified, not false certainty).
M-Pesa access needs an Android appTrue — SMS cannot be read by a web app. The WhatsApp-forwarding interim keeps the pilot viable without native development.
Trust cold-start: owners don't believe an app over their driverThe engine never asks them to. It shows machine-extracted evidence with sources attached — the same statements the owner could request, already read.
